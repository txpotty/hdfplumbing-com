    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Davie

    Small under-sink hydromechanical traps usually land between $1,000 and $3,000 installed, and buried interceptors with excavation commonly run $5,000 to $20,000 or more. The spread comes from access and how much concrete or paving we cut. Permits add a little. We quote in writing after a site visit, with the trap, the labor and the permit fees listed as separate lines.

    For indoor traps we lean on Zurn, Watts, Schier and Endura, because their parts are easy to find and their units carry PDI or ASME certification. Schier's plastic bodies do not rust, which helps in hot, wet kitchens. Brand matters less than sizing. We pick the rating first, then the brand that fits the space and budget.

    You get the manufacturer's warranty on the unit and a written labor warranty from us on the installation. Factory terms vary, so we hand you the registration card or file it for you. Keep your cleaning logs. Makers may ask for service records before paying a claim.

    On a commercial kitchen, no. The work needs a licensed plumber and a permit in nearly every jurisdiction. A homeowner adding a small under-sink trap to a home kitchen may be allowed to do it, but even then the flow control and vent have to be right or the trap siphons and does nothing. Ask the building department first.

    We start with a fixture list and the drain rate of each fixture. Each sink's volume and drain time give a flow rate, and the dish machine's spec sheet gives its flow. That gives the GPM figure. Hydromechanical traps are picked by that GPM rating, while gravity interceptors are sized by volume and retention time from the same data.

    Size comes from flow, and flow comes from your fixtures. We add up the drain rate of each sink compartment, floor sink and dish machine, then apply the code's drain-down time to get gallons per minute. Gravity tanks use volume instead. For those, the local pretreatment program often has its own formula based on seats or fixture units, and we use theirs.

    Watch the pump-out bills. A trap that needs service far more often than it used to is either too small or broken inside. Smell is another clue. Slow floor sinks, grease odor at the trap, a lid that no longer seals, or a failed FOG inspection all point the same way, and we can tell you which it is in one visit.

    In everyday talk the two names get swapped. Strictly, a grease trap is the small indoor unit rated in gallons per minute, and an interceptor is the large tank rated in gallons of capacity. Codes vary on the wording. Some plumbing codes call both of them interceptors and separate them as hydromechanical and gravity, so we use whatever terms your permit office uses.
