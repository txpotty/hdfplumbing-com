    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Four Corners

    Installed prices for small indoor hydromechanical traps typically fall between $1,500 and $5,000. Outdoor tanks cost more, mostly for digging and putting the site back. Brand is a small part of it. The written estimate holds unless the dig turns up a buried surprise.

    For indoor traps we lean on Zurn, Watts, Schier and Endura, because their parts are easy to find and their units carry PDI or ASME certification. Schier's plastic bodies do not rust, which helps in hot, wet kitchens. Brand matters less than sizing. We pick the rating first, then the brand that fits the space and budget.

    In everyday talk the two names get swapped. Strictly, a grease trap is the small indoor unit rated in gallons per minute, and an interceptor is the large tank rated in gallons of capacity. Codes vary on the wording. Some plumbing codes call both of them interceptors and separate them as hydromechanical and gravity, so we use whatever terms your permit office uses.

    Almost always, yes. You will usually need a plumbing permit plus a sign-off from the sewer authority's pretreatment program. We pull the permit, submit the sizing calculation and schedule the rough and final inspections. Expect an inspector on site.

    Zurn and Watts make up a lot of our indoor installs, with Schier and Endura when a plastic body makes more sense. For outdoor work we set precast concrete tanks from nearby casting yards, or polyethylene interceptors when weight or access is a problem. Any certified brand works. What we will not install is an uncertified import trap, because inspectors reject them and the parts disappear after a year.

    An under-sink hydromechanical trap with good access is usually a half-day job, including the flow control and a leak test. Floor-recessed units take longer because we cut and patch concrete. Outdoor tanks are different. A buried interceptor runs two to five working days after permit, mostly driven by the dig and the paving repair.

    Size comes from flow, and flow comes from your fixtures. We add up the drain rate of each sink compartment, floor sink and dish machine, then apply the code's drain-down time to get gallons per minute. Gravity tanks use volume instead. Outdoor tanks get sized by the sewer authority's formula, which we run for you.
