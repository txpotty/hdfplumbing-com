    Fryer volume drives the schedule more than seat count. Plenty of busy restaurants pump monthly. Lighter kitchens, like a coffee shop with a small food menu, can stretch to every two or three months as long as depth readings stay under the 25 percent line.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. In a sewer pipe it sticks to the walls in layers, catches food scraps and wipes, and eventually closes the pipe down to a pencil-sized opening.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Generally a trap is the small unit indoors, often under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    Grease passes the outlet and plugs the line. Backups follow, usually at the worst hour of service. Beyond the mess, many sewer districts fine kitchens for grease discharges and can bill them for jetting the public main, and a health inspector can close a kitchen with sewage on the floor.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. Haulers call it brown grease. It is different from the yellow grease in your used fryer oil bin, which is recycled separately and should never be poured into the trap.
