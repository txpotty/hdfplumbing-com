    Figure $150 to $400 for an indoor trap and $300 to $1,200 for an outdoor interceptor. A recurring schedule lowers each visit. After a long gap the grease is harder to break up, so the same trap costs more than it would on a steady schedule.

    It is the most common pumping standard. When grease and solids reach a quarter of the depth, it is time to pump. Past that point the water moves through too fast for grease to separate, so grease rides out the outlet even though the trap looks like it is working.

    It goes to a permitted receiving facility. Some of it ends up as biofuel or digester feedstock. The rest is dewatered and disposed of under the facility's permit, and every load we haul is logged so the trail runs from your trap to the gate.

    Short term, slow drains and smell. Longer term, a hardened grease cap that can take hours to break up. Hydrogen sulfide from the rotting grease also attacks concrete, so interceptors that go unpumped for years often need new baffles and risers well before their time.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. You do need to keep the records. Installing a new trap or interceptor, or changing the size of one, usually needs a plumbing permit and sometimes a pretreatment program review before the work starts.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. Those AGRUs have a heater and a skimmer wheel that pull grease into a container, and they need the container emptied and the wheel and screen cleaned on a schedule.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. In a sewer pipe it sticks to the walls in layers, catches food scraps and wipes, and eventually closes the pipe down to a pencil-sized opening.
