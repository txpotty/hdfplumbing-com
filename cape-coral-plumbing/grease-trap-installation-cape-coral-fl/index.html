    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Cape Coral

    Installed prices for small indoor hydromechanical traps typically fall between $1,500 and $5,000. Outdoor tanks cost more, mostly for digging and putting the site back. Brand is a small part of it. We give you a written estimate after we walk the site, and it does not change unless we hit something underground nobody could see.

    From first call to final inspection, most indoor jobs take one to two weeks, and most of that is waiting on the permit and the part. The install itself is often a single day. Outdoor jobs run longer. Gravity interceptors can take three to six weeks end to end because of plan review and tank delivery.

    Plan on a permit. Grease interceptors are regulated by both the plumbing code and the local sewer use ordinance, so two offices may want to see the plans. You get copies of every approval for your files. Unpermitted traps cause trouble at lease transfer.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. For a small indoor trap in a busy kitchen, that can be weekly. Outdoor tanks go longer. Many gravity interceptors are pumped every 30 to 90 days, and your local FOG program may set a maximum interval no matter what the trap looks like.

    Two warranties apply here. Manufacturer coverage on the unit varies by brand and material, and plastic bodies usually get the longer terms. Our labor warranty covers the joints and the setting, and you get it in writing up front.

    Plastic hydromechanical traps often last 20 years or more, while coated steel traps can rust out in 7 to 15 depending on how harsh the wastewater runs. A precast interceptor can last a very long time if it is pumped on schedule and the coating holds. Cleaning habits matter most. A trap that is scraped with a metal tool every week or left full of grease for months ages much faster than one that gets regular service.

    That depends on the code your jurisdiction follows and the type of trap. Rules split, since hot water and detergent can carry grease past some indoor units. We check before we pipe it. Food waste disposers are a separate question, and most codes do not allow a disposer to discharge into a grease interceptor at all.

    As close to the grease-producing fixtures as the space allows, so grease does not cool and coat the pipe before it reaches the trap.
