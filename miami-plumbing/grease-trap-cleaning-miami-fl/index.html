    Grease Trap Inspection and Repair in Miami

    An empty trap is the only time anyone can see what is wrong with it. Cracked baffles and missing outlet tees are the usual finds. A missing outlet tee lets the floating grease cap pour straight into the sewer, and it is one of the most common failures we find on older concrete interceptors. We can often fix small problems on the spot.

    Hydro-Jetting for Grease Lines in Miami, FL

    A cable machine pokes a hole through grease. A jetter takes it off the pipe wall. We run a jetter at roughly 3,000 to 4,000 psi with a rotating nozzle that cuts the grease off the full circumference of the pipe and flushes it downstream. Afterward a camera pass confirms the pipe is clean.

    Scheduled Grease Trap Maintenance Plans in Miami

    Most pretreatment programs use the 25 percent rule: pump before floating grease and settled solids together take up a quarter of the trap's liquid depth. Other programs simply set a fixed interval, commonly 90 days for large interceptors. We work to whichever rule applies. Changes are easy.

    Grease Trap Hydro-Jetting in Miami, FL

    Some traps have years of grease baked onto the walls and inlet piping. That crust survives a normal pump-out. Jetting the empty tank and its inlet line strips it back to bare walls and gets back the capacity that crust was taking. Plan on about an extra hour.

    Grease Trap Cleaning Cost in Miami, FL

    Cost follows trap size and fill level. A 20-gallon under-sink trap is a short visit. A 1,500-gallon interceptor that has gone five months without pumping needs a vacuum truck, a pressure wand and extra disposal fees for the volume, so it lands at the high end. Regular pumping keeps the per-visit price down.

    Emergency Grease Trap Services in Miami, FL

    Gray water coming up through the floor sinks in the middle of service is the classic sign the trap has hit capacity. We send a truck the same day when we can.     Under-Sink Grease Trap Maintenance in Miami, FL

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps are rated by flow, usually 10 to 50 gallons per minute, and they hold far less than people expect. In a busy kitchen, that means cleaning every week or two. We take the lid off, remove grease and solids, scrape the baffles, check the flow control fitting on the inlet, and replace the lid gasket if it is flattened or torn. Most odor from these units comes from a bad gasket.
