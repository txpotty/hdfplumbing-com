    Frequently Asked Questions About Rooter Services in University

    Rooting a sink, tub or toilet usually costs $125 to $300. Main sewer rooting through a cleanout usually runs $200 to $500. Adding a jetting pass brings the total to about $450 to $1,000, depending on line length and how much grease is in it. After-hours calls cost more.

    Slow drains, gurgling pipes and sewer smell are the early ones. If the tub fills when the toilet flushes, the main is blocked. That one needs a call. More than one fixture draining slow at the same time usually means the main, not a branch.

    Gurgling toilets, sewage odors and slow drains in several fixtures are the common signs. Water backing up into a tub when you flush is another clue. Take that one seriously. Repeat clogs in one line usually mean a deeper problem, often roots at a joint well out in the yard.

    Rooting is safe with a trained tech and the correct cable and blade. Collapsed or badly cracked pipe can be hurt by anything run through it. Feel matters. We stop if the pipe shows damage, and we bring in the camera to look before going any further down the line.

    Most single-drain clogs take 1 to 3 hours from arrival to flow test. Main lines with heavy roots, or jobs that need a jetting pass, can stretch to half a day or more. Access is the wild card. With no usable cleanout, we pull a toilet to get in, and that adds about an hour.

    Clearing a clog in University does not need a permit. Repair work is different. If the camera shows broken pipe that needs a dig, a new cleanout or a sewer replacement, a plumbing permit is usually required. We pull it and schedule the inspection for you.

    Most rooter jobs include a limited guarantee for a set number of days. If the line clogs again within that time, we return at no extra charge.

    We run RIDGID, Spartan and Gorlitz augers, plus sewer cameras and jetters from the same tier of commercial makers. They survive heavy daily use. Brand matters less than setup. Even a top machine can hurt a pipe with the wrong head, so picking the cutter gets as much attention as the machine.
