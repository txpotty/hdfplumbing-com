    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in University

    Expect roughly $1,500 to $4,000 for a small indoor trap and $7,000 to $20,000 or more for an outdoor gravity interceptor. Recessing an indoor unit into the slab pushes the price up because of saw-cutting and patching. Location drives the rest. Lids under traffic cost more, and so does repaving.

    For indoor traps we lean on Zurn, Watts, Schier and Endura, because their parts are easy to find and their units carry PDI or ASME certification. Schier's plastic bodies do not rust, which helps in hot, wet kitchens. Brand matters less than sizing. A well-known trap installed too small will still fail inspection, so we pick the size first and the brand second.

    It keeps cooking grease out of the public sewer. Hot greasy water cools as it moves through the trap, the grease floats to the top, and cleaner water leaves through the outlet. Grease that reaches the mains hardens into blockages, and the sewer authority bills the kitchens it traces back to. Your own drain lines benefit too.

    Two warranties apply here. Manufacturer coverage on the unit varies by brand and material, and plastic bodies usually get the longer terms. Our labor warranty covers the joints and the setting, and you get it in writing up front.

    Half a day for most indoor units. That covers setting the trap, piping the inlet and outlet, installing the flow control and running a water test. Gravity interceptors take two to five days on site, plus time for the inspector to see the tank before backfill and again at final. Permits add lead time.

    In everyday talk the two names get swapped. The trade usually calls the small indoor unit a trap and the big outdoor tank an interceptor. Codes vary on the wording. Many codes say hydromechanical interceptor and gravity interceptor instead, and we match the local wording on the permit.

    As close to the grease-producing fixtures as the space allows, so grease does not cool and coat the pipe before it reaches the trap. Leave room to open the lid and clean it without moving equipment. Never under a cook line. Outdoor tanks should sit where the hauler can reach them and nothing gets parked on the lids.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. Busy kitchens with small indoor traps often hit that mark every week. Outdoor tanks go longer. A gravity interceptor typically gets pumped monthly to quarterly, and some sewer programs set the schedule for you.
