    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Florida

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

    When the old heater lets go, the first question is always the same. Do you put in another tank, or is this the time to go tankless? National buying guides skip the local conditions, and those conditions decide more than the brochure does. Incoming water temperature, how hot or cold the garage runs and how much mineral is in the water change the answer, sometimes by a lot, so it pays to know them before you sign anything.

    Salt air in coastal parts of Florida corrodes outdoor fittings and exposed metal pipe faster than in inland areas.

    How Each Type Works

    A tank heater stores 40 to 75 gallons and keeps it hot around the clock with a gas burner or electric elements. They are familiar and inexpensive to put in. The tradeoff is standby loss, the energy spent reheating water nobody is using. Once back-to-back showers drain the tank, you wait 30 to 60 minutes while it recovers.

    A tankless unit heats water only when a fixture calls for it. Open a tap and a flow sensor lights the burner, the water picks up heat in the exchanger, and it keeps coming until you shut it off. Capacity is rated in gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. Tank size does not apply. Most whole-house units burn gas, because an electric tankless for a full house often needs more amperage than the panel can spare.

    How Local Conditions Change the Math

    Tankless output depends on how many degrees the heater has to add. Where the cold water coming in is already fairly warm, a unit can push a higher flow rate. In warm climates the incoming water stays mild even in winter. Less temperature rise gives you more flow. Where the supply water is cold most of the year, sizing gets tighter and matters more.

    Garage temperature counts too. A tank in a hot garage loses less heat to standby, which narrows the tankless efficiency edge in summer. Those same hot garages make hybrid heat pump water heaters work very well, since they pull heat out of the surrounding air and cool the garage a little as a side benefit. In a cold basement or garage a heat pump unit struggles.

    Hard water hits tankless units hardest. Most manufacturers void the heat exchanger warranty if the unit is not flushed with descaler every year in hard water. Tank heaters suffer as well, with sediment cutting years off their life and making that popping noise you hear when the burner fires. Either way, a softener or a scale inhibitor cartridge on the cold inlet earns its cost back.

    Water across much of Florida comes from limestone aquifers and runs hard, leaving heavy scale on fixtures and inside water heaters.

    Comparing the Costs

    Prices move with fuel type, venting and code upgrades. These installed ranges are typical:

    • Standard gas or electric tank, 40 to 50 gallons: about $1,400 to $2,800 installed.
    • Whole-house gas tankless: about $3,000 to $6,000 installed, higher when the gas line needs upsizing.
    • Hybrid heat pump tank: about $2,800 to $5,000 installed, and rebates or federal tax credits can take a bite out of that.
    • Yearly tankless descaling: about $150 to $300 if a plumber does it.
    • Tank flush: about $100 to $200, once a year in hard water.

    The gas line is the cost people miss. Most existing lines were sized for a 40,000 BTU tank, and a whole-house tankless wants 180,000 BTU or more. Switching from tank to tankless usually also means new stainless or PVC venting, plus a condensate line if the unit is a condensing model, and a 120 volt outlet for the control board. A good quote lists each of those as its own line item.

    Which One Should You Choose?

    Budget replacements and rentals still call for a tank. It is also the practical pick when the existing gas line and flue would need major work to support a tankless. Tankless is a good fit for bigger families who run out of hot water and for owners planning to stay, plus anyone who wants the floor space back. In an all-electric home with the heater in a warm garage, a hybrid heat pump is worth a serious look. Expect a good tank to last 8 to 12 years on hard water and a maintained tankless 15 to 20.

    When to Call a Professional

    Water heater installs touch gas piping, venting and combustion air, along with the expansion tank and the T and P relief line. Every one of those gets checked at inspection.
