    Why a Sewer Camera Inspection Belongs on Every Florida Home Inspection

    By Andrew Blake · August 13, 2026 · 5 min read

    A general home inspection covers a lot. The inspector checks the roof, the electrical panel, the HVAC and the water heater, along with whatever plumbing is exposed. The inside of the main sewer usually gets skipped. Running water in every fixture for 20 minutes proves very little, because a cracked or root-packed line can drain fine during a short test. A sewer scope is one of the best add-ons a buyer can order before closing.

    What a Sewer Camera Inspection Involves

    The plumber feeds a waterproof camera on a flexible push cable through a cleanout, usually near the foundation or out in the front yard. The video records as the camera moves down the pipe, and you can watch it live on the monitor. Most units have a locating transmitter in the head, so we can paint the exact spot and depth of a problem on the grass. Plan on 30 to 60 minutes. Ask for the video file and a written summary before the plumber leaves.

    No cleanout? We can sometimes go in from a roof vent or through a toilet flange. That access may add to the price. Most residential scopes run $150 to $350 when there is a cleanout in reach.

    Before the visit, ask the listing agent for any records of past sewer work. Root cutting invoices or an old lining warranty are worth having. That history tells the plumber what to look for and whether earlier repairs are holding up. Sellers often have it in a drawer.

    Ask the seller, too, whether the house has ever had a main line backup into the basement or the lowest shower. A yes there tells us to look hard at the first 20 feet out from the foundation. It is a short question worth asking.

    What the Camera Can Reveal

    Sewer problems are expensive, and you rarely see them from inside the house. A camera can turn up:

    • Root intrusion at the joints, common near mature trees.
    • Bellies or sags where the pipe settled and waste collects.
    • Offset or separated joints from years of soil movement.
    • Cracked or collapsed sections in clay tile or cast iron.
    • Heavy corrosion and scale inside older cast iron.
    • Grease buildup that points to ongoing maintenance.
    • Bad past repairs, like two different pipe materials joined without the right coupling.

    Why It Matters for Florida Buyers

    Newer homes across Florida mostly use PEX or CPVC with PVC drains, though copper pinhole leaks are still a frequent repair in older homes.

    Every era of construction brings its own sewer risks. Older neighborhoods may still run on the original clay tile or cast iron. Cast iron under the slab was standard in many 1960s to 1980s houses, and it corrodes from the inside until the bottom of the pipe splits open. Newer ABS and PVC is not immune either. Bad bedding and clay that moves can still sag the line or pull a joint apart.

    Trees add another wrinkle. Shade trees help curb appeal, but their roots go hunting for moisture in the sewer. Rural lots and some edge-of-town properties use septic instead. There the camera shows the condition of the line out to the tank, and that footage goes nicely with a separate septic inspection.

    A high water table can limit how well drain fields work, so many properties here use elevated mound systems or careful maintenance.

    How the Results Help You Negotiate

    Sewer repairs cost real money. Figure $1,500 to $4,500 for a spot repair, and $4,000 to $12,000 or more for a new lateral or a trenchless liner. Finding a problem before closing gives you options. You can ask the seller to fix it or ask for a credit, or walk away. If the line looks good, you get a baseline video to compare against later. Sellers benefit too, since a pre-listing scope lets them fix problems on their own schedule.

    When to Call a Professional

    Book the scope early in your inspection contingency period. That leaves time to get repair quotes if the video shows trouble. Hire a licensed plumber rather than a general inspector, since a plumber can read the footage and price the real repair.
