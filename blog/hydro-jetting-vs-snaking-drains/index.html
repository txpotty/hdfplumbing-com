    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    What Is Actually in the Pipe

    Before we pick a tool, we want to know what the clog is made of. A toilet that stopped after one flush of wipes is a different problem from a kitchen line that has been slowing for a year. Roots and grease respond to different methods. The history tells us a lot.

    A high water table across much of Florida affects drainage, septic design, and how easily crews can dig for underground repairs.

    Ground movement opens joints, and open joints let roots in. A root mass in a sewer line is one of the most common reasons a cable machine comes back out wrapped in fibers. It also tells us a simple clearing will not last. Roots regrow fast.

    Drain Snaking: The Standard First Step

    A drain snake, also called an auger or a cable machine, is a long coiled steel cable with a cutting head on the end. We feed it into the drain, the motor spins it, and the head bores through the clog. Most calls take under an hour. For a sink or tub, a small handheld unit with quarter-inch or 3/8 cable does the job. Main lines get a larger machine with 1/2 or 3/4 inch cable and blades sized to the pipe.

    • Good for: hair, wipes, a single hard blockage, light roots
    • Limits: opens a channel but leaves grease and scale on the pipe walls
    • Price: usually the lower-cost option

    The weakness is that a snake opens a hole but does not clean the pipe. A 4-inch line coated in grease might get a 2-inch channel through the middle, which flows fine for a few weeks. Then the remaining grease catches more debris and the clog comes back. Repeat calls are the sign.

    Hydro Jetting: Cleaning the Whole Pipe

    A hydro jetter pushes water through a hose at 3,000 to 4,000 psi on a typical residential line. Jets on the back of the nozzle drive it up the line and scrub the full circumference of the pipe as it travels. It cuts grease and sludge off the walls. Roots get sliced off flush with the joint.

    Many wells in Florida produce water with a sulfur smell or high iron, which often calls for treatment to protect fixtures and appliances.

    Scale and grease are where jetting really earns its cost. Inside cast iron, mineral scale narrows the bore and gives grease a rough surface to grab. A jetter with a descaling nozzle takes it back close to the original diameter, and the line stays open for a year or more instead of a few weeks. Commercial kitchens jet on a schedule.

    • Good for: grease, sludge, scale, recurring roots
    • Limits: needs a camera check first on old or fragile pipe

    Choosing Between Them

    A first-time clog in a single fixture almost always gets a snake. It is faster and costs less, and usually that is all it takes. A line that clogs again within a few months, or one that a cable cannot get through, gets a camera and then usually a jetter. The camera decides.

    Houses from the late 1970s through the mid 1990s may have polybutylene supply pipe, which many insurers dislike and owners choose to replace.

    Pipe condition limits the choice. Jetting at full pressure inside a cracked clay line or thin, rusted cast iron can make a bad pipe worse. On older lines we camera first, then jet at lower pressure or snake if the pipe is too far gone. PVC and newer cast iron handle full pressure fine.

    Booking the Right Service

    Tell the dispatcher which drains are slow and how long it has been happening. Knowing that, we can usually send the right equipment the first time.
