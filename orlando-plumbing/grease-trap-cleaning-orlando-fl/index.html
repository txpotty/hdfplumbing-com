    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Orlando

    Figure $150 to $400 for an indoor trap and $300 to $1,200 for an outdoor interceptor. Regular service brings the price down. Traps that go a long time between pump-outs take longer to break up and pump, so the same unit can cost noticeably more after a long gap than it would on a steady interval.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    There is no parts warranty on a pump-out, since nothing gets installed. The work itself is guaranteed. Should it drain poorly after our visit, or a backup trace back to something we missed, the crew returns and makes it right.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. The industry calls it brown grease. Yellow grease, the used fryer oil in your collection bin, is a separate product that gets recycled and should never go into the trap.

    It is the most common pumping standard. When grease and solids reach a quarter of the depth, it is time to pump. Past that point the water moves through too fast for grease to separate, so grease rides out the outlet even though the trap looks like it is working.

    It depends on trap size and menu. Commercial kitchens usually land between every two weeks and every three months. A home with a small under-sink unit might only need it once a year, while a busy fryer kitchen with a 50-gallon trap can need it every other week.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. Inside a sewer pipe it builds up in layers on the walls, traps food and wipes, and can shrink the opening to the size of a pencil.

    Under-sink traps take 30 to 60 minutes. Plan one to three hours for an outdoor interceptor. The long end is for big tanks with a hard grease cap, where the crew has to break up the cap with a pressure wand before the suction hose can move it.
